The material footprint of intelligence

Artificial intelligence infrastructure is becoming a new industrial geography. The public interface is a prompt box; the physical system is a chain of semiconductors, fiber routes, substations, transformers, water lines, cooling towers, land leases, backup generation, and tax agreements. This publication treats AI data centers as material infrastructure embedded in local resource systems.

The central mistake in public debate is to discuss AI as if it lives only in software. That framing hides the thermal problem. Every watt used by compute becomes heat. The more dense the hardware, the more urgent the cooling design. GPU clusters built for training and inference can drive rack densities far beyond traditional enterprise server rooms.

PUE

Power Usage Effectiveness: total facility energy divided by IT equipment energy. Lower is better, but it does not reveal water impact.

WUE

Water Usage Effectiveness: water use per unit of IT energy. Essential in arid regions.

CUE

Carbon Usage Effectiveness: emissions tied to energy sourcing. Needed when dry cooling raises electric load.

Cooling is policy

Evaporative cooling can reduce electrical overhead and improve PUE, but it does so by consuming water. Dry cooling can reduce water use, but it may raise electricity demand. Direct-to-chip and immersion systems can reduce water reliance and support high-density racks, but they add capital cost and maintenance complexity. The best local choice depends on the region's binding constraint: water, power, carbon, cost, or reliability.

The Southwest problem

The Southwest is attractive for compute: land, solar resource, open industrial parcels, and state competition for investment. It is also water constrained. That combination creates a political economy problem. The public may be asked to provide land-use approvals, water access, grid upgrades, and tax incentives while the private operator captures the compute value.

New Mexico should not refuse every project. It should refuse vague projects. A serious project should disclose expected peak megawatts, annual energy, water source, annual withdrawal, consumptive use, WUE, PUE, cooling design, drought plan, interconnection requirements, ratepayer impacts, and community benefit terms.

AI infrastructure governance should start with one rule: no public resource commitment without public resource accounting.
