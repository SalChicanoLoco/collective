The Cloud Wants Water

A public-facing article on AI data centers, water, power, and resource accountability in the Southwest.
Salvador Sena · Sena Colectivo · 2026 research draft
HubPeriodicalPolitical briefCalculations

Artificial intelligence is usually sold as something invisible: a chatbot, a cloud service, a prompt box. But the infrastructure underneath it is not invisible. It is land, power, transmission, cooling equipment, tax policy, and water.

That matters in New Mexico and the wider Southwest because the next wave of artificial intelligence is not just a software boom. It is an industrial buildout. Large AI data centers can draw tens or hundreds of megawatts of electricity, concentrate heat in GPU racks, and require continuous cooling. When that cooling uses evaporative systems, the facility can become a major water consumer.

The public question is simple: who gets the compute, who pays for the infrastructure, and whose water absorbs the risk?

The numbers are large enough to move from technology news into public policy. DOE/LBNL reported U.S. data-center electricity use at about 176 terawatt-hours in 2023, with a 2028 estimate range of 325 to 580 terawatt-hours. The International Energy Agency projects global data-center electricity demand around 945 terawatt-hours by 2030.

Water accounting is more complicated because it depends on climate, cooling design, source water, and whether water is withdrawn and returned or consumed through evaporation. That is exactly why disclosure matters. A facility with low Power Usage Effectiveness may still have high Water Usage Effectiveness. A project that looks efficient on electricity can be expensive in water.

New Mexico should not treat AI infrastructure as automatically good or automatically bad. The state should treat it as an industrial land-use decision. Communities should ask for public answers before approvals: expected peak power, water source, annual withdrawal, annual consumption, cooling design, backup generators, transmission upgrades, tax incentives, local hiring, ratepayer exposure, and drought contingency plans.

The strongest projects will be those that use closed-loop cooling, reclaimed or non-potable water, transparent metering, renewable integration, heat reuse where practical, and enforceable community benefits. The weakest projects will ask for cheap land, cheap water, and tax breaks while hiding operational details behind trade-secret claims.

AI may become part of the future economy. But in the Southwest, no future economy is serious if it treats water as an afterthought.

QuestionWhy it matters
How much electricity at peak and annually?Determines grid upgrades, reliability impacts, and ratepayer exposure.
What is the water source?Potable municipal water, groundwater, reclaimed water, and brackish water are not equivalent.
What is the WUE?Shows water consumed per unit of IT energy.
What happens in drought?Data centers require continuous cooling and cannot simply fallow like a field.
