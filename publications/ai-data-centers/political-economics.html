Who Gets the Compute, Who Pays for the Water?

A less technical political brief focused on natural-resource economics, public leverage, and local accountability.
Salvador Sena · Sena Colectivo · 2026 research draft
HubNewspaperPeriodicalCalculations

AI data centers are not just technology projects. In the Southwest they are claims on scarce natural resources. They require land, transmission capacity, water access, road access, tax treatment, backup power, and public tolerance for industrial scale.

The natural-resource economics are straightforward. A private compute company can monetize electricity and water by converting them into AI services sold globally. The community hosting the facility may receive construction jobs, some permanent jobs, and tax-base claims. But the community may also carry opportunity costs: water committed to industrial cooling, grid upgrades, rate pressure, land-use conflicts, and drought risk.

The key public-policy failure is treating water and power as background inputs instead of negotiated public assets. In New Mexico, water has history. It is tied to acequias, agriculture, pueblos, land grants, municipal growth, and intergenerational survival. Any major industrial consumer entering that system must be evaluated as a resource allocation decision, not a ribbon-cutting opportunity.

The political question is not whether AI is useful. The question is whether local people keep any power over the resources that make AI profitable.

A serious economic-development package should include enforceable resource accounting. The facility should publish annual water withdrawal and consumptive use. It should distinguish potable water from reclaimed water. It should disclose WUE, PUE, energy source, peak load, tax incentives, ratepayer exposure, and drought curtailment terms.

Without those terms, the deal structure is upside down: public resources are committed first, private revenue is captured later, and the community is asked to trust that the benefits will trickle back.

The better model is public leverage. If a company wants water, land, and grid access, the community can demand measurable returns: reclaimed-water use, closed-loop cooling, local technical training, public reporting, community broadband or compute credits, heat reuse studies, strict drought clauses, and no hidden ratepayer subsidy.
